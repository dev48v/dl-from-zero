What the number is supposed to mean

A classifier that outputs 0.83 for its top class is making a claim about frequencies: among all the inputs where it says 0.83, it should be right about 83% of the time. That is calibration, and it is a property of the pairing between confidence and outcome, not of either alone. Written out, the calibration error is

CE = Ec | P(correct | confidence = c) − c |

and the first thing to notice is the conditioning. It is not |E[correct] − E[conf]|, which would let a batch of overconfident predictions be paid for by a batch of underconfident ones. It is per confidence level, and a real network emits a different confidence for every input, so the conditioning event has probability zero and the quantity is not estimable without a decision about what counts as “the same confidence”.

That decision is the bin count. Every practical calibration number — ECE, MCE, reliability diagrams, adaptive ECE — is the same formula evaluated after grouping the inputs, and grouping can only ever cancel:

a bin holding one input  at conf 0.9, true 0.5   contributes 0.4
a bin holding two inputs at conf 0.9, true 0.5 and 0.9 → |0.7 − 0.9| = 0.2

Coarsen and the number falls; refine (into nested bins) and it rises. So the reported ECE is a lower bound on the calibration error at any finer grouping, and how much lower is a reporting choice. On this page the whole ladder is visible at once because p* is known: one bin, fifteen bins, two hundred bins, and one group per distinct confidence value.

The top rung deserves a warning. With 300 distinct confidences, conditioning on the confidence is conditioning on the input, so the finest rung equals Ex|c(x) − p*(ŷ|x)| — the per-input probability error. That is the strictest thing the definition can mean and it is no longer only about calibration; it includes the part of the error a global rescaling was never going to fix. The point is not that this rung is the “true” value. The point is that the definition does not name a rung, the reader assumes the strict one, and the writer reports the loose one.

Where the overconfidence comes from

Nothing about a softmax makes it overconfident. Fit the same network to the exact conditional distribution and it lands on p* and is calibrated to three decimal places — the control on the MEASURE tab. What produces overconfidence is minimising NLL against hard labels on a finite sample. The empirical conditional distribution of a label you observed once is a point mass, so every gradient step pushes the probability of the observed class towards 1, and the only thing stopping it is capacity and the other examples that share the same region. Train longer and the model keeps buying training NLL with confidence it has not earned: on this page, accuracy peaks early and then drifts down 7.7% while the true NLL triples and the temperature that would fix it climbs from 1.05 to 6.78.

This is why the classic figure (Guo et al. 2017) has two curves going opposite ways, and why early stopping on accuracy and early stopping on NLL do not stop at the same place.

One parameter, and why it is the good one

Temperature scaling divides the logits by a single positive scalar before the softmax:

qk(x) = exp(zk(x)/T) / ∑j exp(zj(x)/T)

Dividing by a positive number cannot reorder the logits, so argmax is untouched at every input and the accuracy is unchanged — not approximately, bit-identically. T is fitted by minimising NLL on held-out data, and the objective is best written in β = 1/T:

L(β) = mean over held-out of  [ logsumexp(βz) − βzy ]
dL/dβ   = mean of  [ Eq(β)[z] − zy ]
d²L/dβ² = mean of  Varq(β)[z]   ≥ 0, always

The second derivative is a variance, so it is a sum of squares: the objective is convex in β, there is exactly one optimum, and Newton reaches it in a handful of steps from any start. (Convex in β, not in T — a detail worth keeping, since a bisection over T will still work but a Newton step in T can walk off.)

The stationarity condition dL/dβ = 0 says something readable: the mean logit of the observed class must equal the mean logit the model expects. Overconfidence means the model's expected logit sits above what happens, so β drops below 1 and T rises above it.

And why you may not fit it on the training set

Everyone knows you need a validation split. The reason is sharper than “overfitting”. Scaling every logit by β is exactly scaling the last layer's weights and bias by β, which is a direction the optimiser already had. So on the training set

dLtrain/dβ at β=1  =  ⟨∇W₂L, W₂⟩ + ⟨∇b₂L, b₂⟩

— a directional derivative that training has been driving towards zero for every step it ran. Verified on this page to 1e-17. The training set has no information about the temperature left, because it was all spent. Measured, the training-set fit returns T ≈ 0.91 while the held-out fit on the same logits asks for 1.60, and in 26 of 27 configurations the training-set fit comes back at T ≤ 1 — it wants to make an overconfident model more confident.

Four things wrong with the number that scores it

1. A useless model can score a perfect zero. Take the class marginal p(y), ignore the input entirely, and emit it for everything. Confidence is the largest marginal probability; accuracy is the frequency of that class; those are the same number by definition, so the calibration error is exactly 0 at every grouping — while the NLL is 0.46 nats worse than the oracle and the distance from p* is 0.400. Calibration is a constraint that the trivial predictor satisfies perfectly, which is why it can never be a quality score.

2. The apparatus often adds nothing. Before any recalibration, all nine occupied bins point the same way, so 15 bins reports the same value as one bin — here they agree to 1.9e-16. Every reliability diagram of an uncalibrated network is showing you conf − acc with extra steps.

3. It goes blind exactly when you fix it. After a temperature fit the net signed error is pushed to ~0 by construction, and what remains changes sign across the confidence range — on this page 6 bins overconfident and 4 underconfident, half the population weight each. Coarse bins cancel that; the 15-bin reading is 17.6× smaller than the sum of the per-bin magnitudes. So the reported error falls 40% while the finest rung falls 16%, and over 27 configurations the reported drop flattered the real one 27 times out of 27, with three where the finest rung actually got worse.

4. It has a floor nobody quotes. Run the estimator on p* itself, whose error is exactly zero, and it reports 0.093 at n=100, 0.045 at n=500 and 0.010 at n=5000 with 15 bins. Binned ECE is biased upward on finite samples because each bin's accuracy is a noisy average and the absolute value keeps the noise. Any improvement smaller than that floor is not measurable with that much data — and the improvement this page's own 500-example held-out set would have reported for temperature scaling, 0.026, is smaller than the 0.045 floor.

So what should be reported

A proper scoring rule — NLL or Brier — has none of these problems. It needs no bins, it is minimised uniquely by p*, and it cannot be gamed by a predictor that ignores its input. Scored as rankers over 108 trained models against the exact population NLL, held-out NLL orders the pairs at AUC 0.951 and Brier at 0.909, while ECE-15 manages 0.712 — worse than the plain error rate at 0.791. ECE improves to 0.875 on the one comparison it was designed for (the same model at different temperatures, accuracy held fixed) and reverses 6.5% of its own rankings when the bin count moves from 15 to 10, and 8.6% when the bins go from equal-width to equal-mass.

None of which makes temperature scaling a bad method. It halves the excess NLL here (0.1905 → 0.0955 nats above the irreducible floor) for one parameter and no accuracy cost, and that is a real result measured against the truth. The unreliable part is the instrument, not the fix: report the proper score, report which grouping you used, and report what your estimator reads on a perfectly calibrated reference at your sample size.

The setup, so the numbers can be checked

  • Process. 300 discrete inputs with weights w(x) summing to 1; features in R⁶; p*(y|x) from a 3-hidden-unit tanh teacher over 3 classes with logits divided by τ=0.6. Bayes accuracy 0.7706, conditional entropy 0.5949 nats — the floor no model can beat.
  • Model. 6 → 16 tanh → 3 softmax, 163 parameters, hand-written backward pass, Adam at 0.03. Because the teacher has 3 hidden units and the student has 16, the student can represent p* — so the population-trained control is a fair control and not a straw man.
  • Everything exact. Accuracy is ∑ w(x) p*(ŷ(x)|x), not a count. NLL is ∑ w(x) ∑ p* log(1/q), not an average over draws. The only place sampling appears is where it belongs: the training labels, the held-out sets used to fit T, and the estimator noise floor.

🔨 How it is built

1. A finite input space, so “the calibration error” exists

makeProcess()  → { X[300], W[300] summing to 1, P[300][3] = p*(y|x) }

This is the decision the whole page rests on. If x were continuous, every expectation would be a Monte-Carlo estimate and a claim like “the estimator reports 0.045 for something whose true value is 0” would be an estimate about an estimate. With a finite, weighted input space every population quantity is a for loop, and the calibration error at any grouping is exactly computable. The teacher is a network rather than a linear map so that misspecification is a choice (shrink H) rather than a permanent feature.

2. One function for every calibration metric on the page

groupCE(conf, acc, w, gid)   // sum_g | sum_g w·acc − sum_g w·conf |
// gid = () => 0                    → the confidence gap
// gid = i => binIndex(conf[i], B)   → ECE-B, equal width
// gid = i => quantileBin[i]         → ECE-B, equal mass
// gid = i => conf[i]                → the finest grouping

Writing them as one function with a grouping argument is what turns “ECE depends on the binning” from a caveat into a table. It also makes the lower-bound property obvious rather than surprising: coarser gid, fewer absolute values, smaller sum.

3. The two zero rows, which are identities and not experiments

metrics(pr, pr.P).ce === 0                    // exactly, at every grouping
metrics(pr, constProbs(pr, pr.marg)).ce < 1e-15  // one group: conf === P(argmax marginal)

The oracle's zero is bit-exact because acc[i] and conf[i] are literally the same number, so the two sums are the same floating-point computation. The marginal predictor's is 1e-16 rather than 0 for one honest reason: its single group's confidence is summed as ∑ wi·c while the marginal was summed as ∑ wi·p*, and those are the same value by algebra but not the same rounding. Asserting < 1e-15 rather than === 0 is the difference between a test and a wish.

4. Tie handling, which is a real bug in real code

// equal-mass bins: fill until the quota, then close the bin
if (k < B-1 && cum >= tot*(k+1)/B && (splitTies || conf[next] !== conf[i])) k++;

The naive version splits a run of identical confidences across bins to hit its quota. Do that to the marginal predictor — whose confidences are all the same number — and the metric reports 0.066 for a predictor whose calibration error is provably zero, because each arbitrary slice has a different accuracy. Both versions are on the page behind a dropdown, because a metric that depends on your sort order is worth seeing once.

5. Newton in β, with the second derivative written as a variance

d1 += w * (E_q[z] − z_target)
d2 += w * sum_k q[k] * (z[k] − E_q[z])^2      // NOT E[z²] − E[z]²

Both forms are algebraically the variance; only the second is a sum of squares in floating point. That is what lets the page assert d2 ≥ 0 at every β with no tolerance at all, which is the numerical statement of “this objective is convex”. The fitted β is then confirmed against a 0.001-step grid over the whole range, because Day 67 and Day 68 were both nearly wrong from trusting a coarse search.

6. The training-set identity, so the warning is a measurement

scaleDirDeriv(M, items) = ⟨∇W₂L, W₂⟩ + ⟨∇b₂L, b₂⟩
assert |tempDerivs(trainItems, 1).d1 − scaleDirDeriv(M, trainItems)| < 1e-9   // 1e-17

“Don't fit the temperature on the training set” is usually asserted. Here it is derived: the two quantities are the same directional derivative, computed by two different code paths, and they agree to 1e-17. That is why the training-set fit returns ~0.91 no matter how the model is trained, and why it is below 1 in 26 of 27 configurations.

7. Sign counts, because the mechanism has to be visible

signCounts(pr, Q, B) → { over, under, wOver, wUnder, net, abs }
assert signCounts(before).under === 0             // one sign, so 15 bins === 1 bin
assert signCounts(after).abs / signCounts(after).net > 10
for (B of [1,2,15,50,200]) assert signCounts(·, B).net === |conf − acc|

The last line is the one that makes the page's argument airtight: the net reading is independent of the bin count, so everything the bin count changes is cancellation. Without it, “binning hides sign-varying error” is a story; with it, it is arithmetic.

8. What the tests pin

// the two perfect scores, as identities
assert(metrics(pr, pr.P).ce === 0 at every B and both schemes);
assert(margPredictor.ce < 1e-15 && margPredictor.nll === entropy(marginal));

The last group is the one to steal. Three days running, this series has found that the diagnostic named after the problem is a poor ranker of the problem — Day 68's exposure gap at AUC 0.529, Day 69's one-step cache check at recall 0.500, and now ECE at 0.712 against an error rate that manages 0.791. The pattern is not that the metrics are wrong. It is that a metric built to be interpretable gets quoted as though it were decisive, and nobody scores it, because scoring it requires a ground truth that only a synthetic process has.
