DEEPLEARNINGFROMZERO · DAY 79

🎲 Dropout at inference — you train with dropout, turn it off at test time, and the whole network answers. MC dropout says leave it on, run k forward passes and average. Both are approximations to the same object, the mean of the dropout ensemble, and this computes that object exactly instead of approximating it: 12 dropout units means 2⁷¹² = 4096 masks, every one enumerated, so the ensemble mean and variance are sums over a complete set rather than Monte Carlo estimates. Five independently seeded networks carry every claim. 🔴 Turning dropout off does not give you the ensemble mean. The gap reaches 0.5397 on the main network against a typical output magnitude of 0.5537 — and the control pins the cause exactly: make the layer after dropout linear and the gap collapses to 2.75×10⁻¹⁴, floating-point zero, in all five networks. 🔴 And the MC-dropout run people actually do is worse than not doing it. The break-even is σ²/bias² = 13.9 passes here (5.8 to 29.8 across the five), and the measured curve agrees: at k=5 the averaged answer is 69% further from the truth than simply switching dropout off, at k=10 still 18% further, and only at k=30 does it win. 🔴 🔴 The number it reports as uncertainty is a function of a hyperparameter you chose during training. Move the keep probability from 0.5 to 0.95 and the reported spread falls 3.89× while the model’s actual output is bit-identical at every one of the 61 inputs. Same network, same input, same answer, a quarter of the “uncertainty”.

Inverted dropout, so the comparison is fair. A kept unit is divided by the keep probability, which makes the expected post-dropout activation equal the plain activation. That is precisely why “turn dropout off at test time” is supposed to give you the ensemble mean — and it is why the gap below is a real discrepancy rather than a missing rescale.

1. The gap is the nonlinearity, and the control proves it

Weight scaling is exact when the thing after dropout is linear, because the expectation passes straight through. Put a ReLU there and it stops being exact, and the last column is the evidence: with the same weights, the same masks and the same code path, removing that one nonlinearity drops the discrepancy from 0.25–0.83 to about 10⁻¹⁴. That is what makes this a property of the architecture rather than a bug in the enumeration — a bug would not switch itself off when the ReLU does.

2. How many passes before averaging is worth it

🔴 The k-sample mean is unbiased, so its RMS error is σ/√k; the deterministic pass has no variance but a fixed bias. Setting them equal gives k* = σ²/bias², which is 13.9 for this network, and the measured curve crosses exactly where it should. The point is not that 30 passes is expensive — it is that 5 and 10, the numbers that actually appear in code, sit on the wrong side of the line. Averaging a handful of stochastic passes is a more elaborate way of being further from the answer.

3. The uncertainty moves; the prediction does not

🔴 The keep probability is a training hyperparameter. Sweeping it from 0.5 to 0.95 changes the reported spread by 3.89× — and the deterministic output is bit-identical at all 61 inputs, because that pass never sees a mask at all. So the model’s answer and the confidence attached to it come from different places: one from the weights, the other from a number chosen during training. Two teams who picked 0.5 and 0.9 will report different uncertainties for the same prediction from the same network, and neither is more right.

4. What I expected to find, and did not

The hypothesis going in was that this spread is just a restatement of how large the activations are — that it would track the output magnitude and carry nothing else. In four of the five networks it does, at r = 0.73, 0.96, 0.96 and 0.71. In the fifth it is −0.19. One counterexample out of five is not a rounding error, so that claim is not established and is not being made here.

What the table does show is a cleaner negative. The spread does not tell you where the deterministic approximation is worst: correlating it against the actual gap gives −0.33 to +0.83 across the five networks — not merely weak, but with an unstable sign. Whatever this number is measuring, it is not the size of the error it is standing next to.

What this covers, and what it does not

These networks are not trained: the weights are generated deterministically from a stated seed, so nothing here is a statement about what dropout does to a fitted model. There is one dropout layer, one scalar input and one scalar output. There is no label noise anywhere, so the spread measured here is purely the ensemble's own disagreement and there is no aleatoric term to separate it from. A “stochastic forward pass” in section 2 is an index into the enumerated mask outputs — the same distribution a real run would draw from, but the page never executes a fresh forward pass per sample. And none of this evaluates MC dropout as a calibration method: no labels, no reliability diagram, no comparison against an ensemble that was actually trained. The claims are about the arithmetic relationship between three things that are often assumed to be the same.

How it works

// every mask, not a sample of them
for (let m = 0; m < 4096; m++) {
  for (let i = 0; i < 12; i++) drop[i] = (m >> i) & 1 ? h[i] / keep : 0;
  out[m] = head(net, drop);
}

// the control: with a linear head the expectation passes straight through,
// so E[head(drop)] == head(E[drop]) == the dropout-off pass, exactly.
y += w3[j] * (linear ? z : relu(z));

// and the crossover, solved rather than eyeballed
breakEven = variance / (bias * bias);   // sigma^2/k < bias^2

Self-check

Runs on load. The controls do the work here: a claim that two things differ is worth nothing without the configuration in which they provably coincide, so the linear-head case is checked to agree to 1e-12, and the enumeration is checked to be a complete 4096 rather than a sample of it.
