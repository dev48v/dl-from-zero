Rotary position embeddings — absolute positions going in, relative positions coming out, and a decay curve that is mostly noise.

Rotate the query, rotate the key, and the dot product forgets where they were

RoPE does something that sounds impossible. It injects each token's absolute position into the query and key vectors — and yet the attention score between any two tokens depends only on the distance between them. Move a whole sentence ten tokens later and every score inside it is unchanged.

The mechanism is rotation. Split the vector into 2-D pairs, and rotate pair i of the token at position m by angle mθi. Because a dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the difference of their angles, mθ − nθ = (m−n)θ, the absolute part cancels.

The frequency ladder

Each 2-D pair gets its own rotation speed, theta_i = base^(-2i/d). The first pairs spin fast and encode fine, local distinctions; the last pairs spin so slowly that across the entire training window they barely turn at all.

The identity, in one line

Write a 2-D pair of the query at position m as a complex number q, and the key at n as k. RoPE multiplies them by eimθ and einθ. The real part of the Hermitian product is the dot product, and 

Re[ (q e^(i m theta)) * conj(k e^(i n theta)) ]
  = Re[ q conj(k) e^(i (m - n) theta) ]

The absolute positions appear only as their difference. That is the whole theorem, and everything else on this page is a consequence of it.

What it costs

Nothing is learned and nothing is stored: the rotation is computed from the position on the fly. There is no embedding table to run off the end of, which is why RoPE models can be asked about positions past their training length — the next section is about what happens when you do.

The decay curve, and why the obvious way to plot it is noise

People say attention under RoPE decays with distance. Plot it carelessly and you get a flat line of random scatter, because two independent random vectors have an expected dot product of zero at every distance — the curve you drew was sampling noise, not decay.

Averaging the magnitude instead does not rescue it. The table below measures that too, and it is also flat: a random query and a random key are no more and no less aligned at distance 256 than at distance 1.

The decay is real, and it is a statement about a fixed vector. Score a query against itself across a growing offset and the pairs, which started perfectly in phase, drift out of step at different rates until their cosines cancel. That column collapses. It is dephasing, not attenuation, and it is the only one of the three that shows the effect people are describing.

Going past the training length

It is tempting to blame the fast pairs, but they were already wrapping constantly inside the training window - another twenty turns is nothing new. The pair that leaves familiar ground is the slowest one. Its period is longer than the whole context, so across training it only ever swept a small arc; push the position eightfold and it swings into angles that never once appeared during training.

Two standard repairs: position interpolation squeezes the new range back into the old one by scaling positions down; NTK-aware scaling raises the base instead, stretching the slow end's period so the same absolute position lands at the same angle.
