🧩 Weight averaging — averaging the weights of several networks and averaging their predictions are two different operations, and only one of them is an operation on functions. The second is a theorem: under a squared loss the mean member's risk splits exactly into the ensemble's risk plus the spread of the members' predictions, so averaging predictions can never cost more than the mean member — checked here as an equation, closing to 2.4e-17. The first has no such guarantee, and the reason is that a network's weights are one arbitrary representative of a whole orbit computing the identical map. The task is a teacher network over an enumerated input space of 64 points, 48 of them shown to a 41-parameter student with a fixed per-point offset, so the score is an exact population risk and the floor is a computation: the teacher scores exactly 0. THE headline: permuting the five hidden units and flipping their signs gives 3,840 weight vectors that compute the same function to 4.4e-16 — and averaging the network with each of them gives 3,840 different models spanning 0.0249 to 1.9098, a factor of 77, of which exactly one returns the network you started with. So “average the two models” is not a question you can ask about two models. And the average of a network with an equivalent copy of itself is literally a pruned copy of it: flip every sign of a unit and the two copies cancel to exactly zero, which is why deleting the right 4 of 5 units scores 2.94× better than the network they came from. What the two averages differ BY is curvature, and nothing else: shrink the deviations by a half and the gap between them falls by 4.005, then 4.003, 4.001, 4.001 — and 4 to within 2e-9 in the bilinear twin, where the discrepancy has a closed form, while a twin that is affine in what is being averaged has no gap at all, to 7.3e-16. Averaging the iterates of one run needs a floor, and a realizable task has none: at a solution that interpolates the training set every minibatch gradient is exactly 0.0e+0 over all 6 minibatches, so the noise switches itself off and averaging the tail is 1.71× worse than just keeping the last iterate. Give it an offset and the same recipe is 1.11× better — while its training loss is 1.34× worse. Two runs are the hard case and nothing you can compute predicts it: the midpoint of two independent solutions costs 3.57× the better of them and the best of 32 pairs still costs 1.25×, while over the pool the best candidate scores AUC 0.677 against a coin's 0.500 and the weight distance everyone reaches for scores 0.661, falling to 0.589 inside one learning rate. 3 · AVERAGING WITH A SIGN FLIP IS DELETING UNITS

Take the sign-flipped copy of a unit and average: (w + (−w))/2 is exactly zero, so every flipped unit's incoming and outgoing weights vanish. The average of two networks computing the identical function is the same network with those units deleted — which settles, in one line, whether averaging equivalent models can be a no-op. Delete all of them and what is left is a constant, and a constant cannot beat the best constant there is.

Two operations that share a word

“Averaging models” names two things. One takes M networks, runs all of them, and averages the numbers that come out. The other takes the same M networks, averages their weight vectors, and runs the single network that results. The first costs M forward passes forever; the second costs one. That is the whole reason anybody does the second one, and it is why stochastic weight averaging, model soups and exponential moving averages all exist.

The first has a guarantee. Under a squared loss, for any M predictors and any target, the mean member's risk splits exactly:

mean_m risk(f_m)  =  risk(mean_m f_m)  +  mean_m mean_x (f_m(x) - fbar(x))^2

The last term is a mean of squares, so it cannot be negative, so the ensemble is never worse than the average member and is better by exactly the spread. That is the ambiguity decomposition, and the MEASURE tab checks it as an equation — it closes to 2.4e-17, which is rounding and not agreement.

The second has no guarantee at all, and the reason is not that it is a harder theorem. It is that averaging weights is not an operation on functions.

The average of two models is not a function of the two models

Take a trained network and swap two hidden units, carrying their incoming weights, their bias and their outgoing weight along together. Nothing about the computed function changes — the MEASURE tab checks that the outputs move by at most 4.4e-16, which is the summation order and nothing else. Take a unit and negate its incoming weights, its bias and its outgoing weight. Because tanh is odd, that too changes nothing.

With five hidden units that is 5! ⋅ 2⁵ = 3,840 weight vectors, all of them the same function. Average the network with each of them and you get 3,840 different models, spanning a factor of 77 in risk. Exactly one of them — the identity — hands the network back, and it does so bit for bit, because (p+p)/2 is p.

So the question “what do you get if you average these two models?” has no answer. It depends on which representative of each orbit you were handed, and training hands you one at random. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that.

The sharpest case: averaging is pruning

Flip every sign of one unit and average with the original. The incoming weights are (w + (-w))/2 = 0, exactly, and so is the outgoing weight. The unit is gone. So the average of a network with an equivalent copy of itself is that network with a subset of its units deleted — and on the default setting, deleting the right four of the five units scores 2.94× better than the network they came from, because that network is overfitting its 48 training points with 41 parameters. Delete all five and what is left is a constant function, which scores worse than the best constant, as it must.

What the two averages differ by is curvature

If the model is affine in the parameters being averaged, the two operations are the same operation: f(mean w) = mean f(w) by linearity. The MEASURE tab runs that as a control — freeze the first layer, train only the output block, and the two averages agree pointwise to under 1e-15, with the same risk to the last few bits.

Away from that, the difference is the second-order term and nothing else. Scatter parameter vectors around a centre with deviations summing to zero and shrink them by a half: the weight average does not move, and the gap between the averages falls by 4.005, 4.003, 4.001, 4.001. In the twin with the nonlinearity removed — where the model is exactly bilinear in its two blocks — the ratio is 4 to within 2e-9 and the discrepancy has a closed form, -¼ Δw₂ ⋅ (ΔV x), which the page checks to under 2e-15.

That is the honest version of “weight averaging approximates ensembling”. It does, to first order, and the error is the curvature times the square of how far apart the members are. Iterates from one run are close together, so it is a good approximation. Two independent solutions are not, so it is not.

Averaging iterates needs a floor, and a realizable task has none

SWA's premise is that the run has stopped improving and is hovering inside a floor, so the iterates are samples around a centre worth finding. Whether that is true is decided by something you can check without training anything. At a solution that interpolates the training set every residual is zero, so every minibatch gradient is exactly zero — the page reports 0.0e+0 across all six minibatches, not something small. SGD's noise is the disagreement between minibatch gradients; if they are all zero there is no noise, no hovering, and no floor. The run simply keeps descending more and more slowly, and averaging the tail can only average in the past.

That is what the offset sweep measures. With no offset the task is realizable and averaging is 1.71× worse than keeping the last iterate. Put an offset on the labels, so the student can no longer interpolate, and the same recipe becomes 1.11× better. The rate sweep is the same control on the other axis: at the smallest rate the iterates barely spread, the disagreement share is 11.4× smaller than at the largest, and averaging loses.

One number on that card is worth staring at. The averaged network has a worse training loss than the last iterate, by 1.34×, and a better population risk, by 1.11×. If you were watching the loss you were optimising, averaging looks like a mistake.

Two runs, and the element you align to

Between two independently trained networks there is a barrier: the midpoint costs 3.57× the better of them here. Aligning first is the known fix, and the standard recipe picks the group element minimising the distance between the weight vectors. The page runs that exhaustively over all 3,840 elements — and then also runs the search the recipe is a proxy for, minimising the risk of the average.

They are not the same element. Minimising the distance gives a midpoint of 0.1219, worse than either endpoint. Minimising the risk gives 0.0373, better than both, with a barrier of exactly zero — the two runs really are linearly connected once you pick the right representative, and the usual recipe does not pick it. Scored as a ranker over those 3,840 elements the distance manages 0.661 against a coin's 0.500: better than nothing, and a long way from the thing it stands in for.

And nothing predicts it in advance

Over a pool of 32 pairs the best candidate for “what will averaging these two cost?” scores AUC 0.677 against a coin's 0.500, and inside one learning rate it falls to 0.563. The distance between the weight vectors — the number everyone reaches for — scores 0.661 and 0.589. The same distance after aligning scores 0.581 overall and 0.429 within a cell, which is below the coin, because aligning changes which question is being asked: against the aligned average, that same number scores 0.748.

Meanwhile averaging the weights of two independent runs never beat the better member, on 0 of 32 pairs, costing between 1.25× and 8.90×. Averaging their predictions cost no more than the mean member on 32 of 32 — it cannot, that is the theorem — but beat the better member on only 21 of 32, which is the distinction the guarantee is usually quoted past.

What to take from it

  • Averaging predictions is safe by a theorem and costs M forward passes. Averaging weights is free at inference and safe by nothing.
  • They agree to first order. The error is curvature times the square of the spread, so the substitution is sound for iterates of one run and unsound for separate runs.
  • Before averaging a tail, check whether the run has a floor. If the model can interpolate its training set, the minibatch gradients go to exactly zero and there is nothing to average.
  • Watch the risk you care about, not the loss you are optimising: here averaging made the training loss 1.34× worse and the population risk 1.11× better.
  • If you align two runs before averaging, remember the distance is a proxy. Here it found an element 3.27× worse than the one an exhaustive search over the same group found.

The whole thing, from scratch

One file, no libraries, no build step. A two-layer tanh network with a hand-written forward and backward pass, a deterministic generator, and an input space small enough to enumerate so that nothing here is estimated.

1 · One flat parameter vector

Every derivative, every average and every symmetry below indexes the same array, which is what makes “average the weights” a one-line operation and the orbit search a loop.

function psize(h, d){ return h * d + h + h + 1; }   // [ W1 | b1 | w2 | b2 ]
function iW1(h, d, j, k){ return j * d + k; }
function ib1(h, d, j){ return h * d + j; }
function iw2(h, d, j){ return h * d + h + j; }
function ib2(h, d){ return h * d + h + h; }

2 · Forward, with the nonlinearity in exactly one place

Putting act and its derivative in one function each means the linear twin of the whole engine is a two-token edit rather than a second implementation to keep in sync.

function act(z, lin){ return lin ? z : Math.tanh(z); }
function dact(a, lin){ return lin ? 1 : 1 - a * a; }

function forwardOne(p, h, d, x, lin){
  var a = [], j, k, y = p[ib2(h, d)];
  for (j = 0; j < h; j++){
    var z = p[ib1(h, d, j)];
    for (k = 0; k < d; k++) z += p[iW1(h, d, j, k)] * x[k];
    a[j] = act(z, lin);
  }
  for (j = 0; j < h; j++) y += p[iw2(h, d, j)] * a[j];
  return { y: y, a: a };
}

3 · Backward, by hand

var gy = 2 * e / M;                       // d(mean square error)/dy
g[ib2(h, d)] += gy;
for (j = 0; j < h; j++){
  g[iw2(h, d, j)] += gy * f.a[j];
  var gz = gy * p[iw2(h, d, j)] * dact(f.a[j], lin);
  g[ib1(h, d, j)] += gz;
  for (k = 0; k < d; k++) g[iW1(h, d, j, k)] += gz * x[k];
}

4 · Checked against forward mode, not a difference

Dual numbers carry the value and one derivative together. One pass per parameter is expensive and exact: no step size, nothing to tune, and the opposite traversal to the backward pass. The check runs at initialisation — at convergence the true gradient has decayed to nothing and a difference test starts measuring its own floor rather than the code.

5 · The two averages

function meanVec(list){                    // average the PARAMETERS, then run ONCE
  var P = list[0].length, out = [], i, m;
  for (i = 0; i < P; i++){
    var s = 0;
    for (m = 0; m < list.length; m++) s += list[m][i];
    out[i] = s / list.length;
  }
  return out;
}
function meanPred(preds){                   // run ALL of them, then average the outputs
  var N = preds[0].length, out = [], i, m;
  for (i = 0; i < N; i++){
    var s = 0;
    for (m = 0; m < preds.length; m++) s += preds[m][i];
    out[i] = s / preds.length;
  }
  return out;
}

6 · The symmetry group

A permutation of the units and a sign flip per unit. Both are exact symmetries of the function, and neither is a symmetry of the average.

function applySym(p, h, d, perm, signs){
  var q = p.slice(), j, k;
  for (j = 0; j < h; j++){
    var src = perm[j], sg = signs ? signs[j] : 1;
    for (k = 0; k < d; k++) q[iW1(h, d, j, k)] = sg * p[iW1(h, d, src, k)];
    q[ib1(h, d, j)] = sg * p[ib1(h, d, src)];
    q[iw2(h, d, j)] = sg * p[iw2(h, d, src)];
  }
  q[ib2(h, d)] = p[ib2(h, d)];             // the output bias is outside the group
  return q;
}

With h = 5 the whole group is 3,840 elements, so the best alignment is found rather than approximated by a matching heuristic — and the same loop can be run against the risk instead of the distance, which is how the page prices the proxy.

7 · The one check that decides whether averaging a tail is worth doing

No training involved. Put the true function into the student, and look at the minibatch gradients.

for (b = 0; b + batch <= n; b += batch){
  var idx = [];
  for (i = 0; i < batch; i++) idx.push(b + i);
  gmax = Math.max(gmax, maxAbs(gradOf(p, h, d, task.Xtr, task.Ttr, false, idx).g));
}

If that comes back exactly zero, the model can interpolate, the noise switches itself off, and there is no floor for the iterates to hover in.

8 · What the page asserts

The self-check is written as identities wherever an identity exists, and as a derived rounding bound — a multiple of the machine epsilon times a scale computed from the data — wherever floating point is the only thing in the way. A hand-picked threshold is how a page ships red: an idempotent operation re-applied costs about one unit in the last place, and an assertion that it costs zero is a bug in the assertion.

ck(o.probe0.gmax === 0,
   "a minibatch gradient at an interpolating solution is not exactly zero (" + o.probe0.gmax + ")");
ck(o.orbit.identity !== null && o.orbit.identity.L === o.orbit.L0,
   "averaging the network with itself did not return the network exactly");
ck(Math.abs(amb.gap) <= 64 * eps * Math.max(amb.scale, 1),
   "the ambiguity decomposition does not close (" + amb.gap + ")");
ck(o.order.maxCentre <= 64 * eps * Math.max(1, o.orbit.scale),
   "the weight average moved when the deviations were shrunk (" + o.order.maxCentre + ")");
