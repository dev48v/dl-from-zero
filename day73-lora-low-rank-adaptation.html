What an adapter actually is

A LoRA adapter is one line added to one matrix multiply: 

y = Wx  →  y = Wx + (α/r)·B(Ax)

W is frozen. A is r×d, B is d×r, and r is much smaller than d, so the pair holds r(m+n) numbers where the matrix holds mn. B starts at zero, which makes the whole branch output identically zero, which makes the adapted model bit-identical to the base model before training — not close, identical, because adding 0.0 to a float is exact. That is the first card's zero, and it is the reason an adapter can be shipped next to a frozen model without changing it.

Everything else about LoRA follows from a fact that is easy to state and easy to under-use: the product (α/r)BA is a d×d matrix of rank at most r, so an adapter is exactly a restriction on the change, ΔW, not a restriction on the model. The loss surface is untouched. Differentiate through it and you get the whole backward pass in two lines: 

G  = ∂loss/∂(W + ΔW)      ← the ordinary full-matrix gradient
dA = (α/r)·BᵀG       dB = (α/r)·G·Aᵀ

There is no separate LoRA gradient. There is the gradient the matrix would have had, contracted with the two factors. That is worth holding onto, because it says what an adapter does to optimisation: from B = 0 the second line is the only one that can move — dA contains Bᵀ and B is zero, so the first step cannot rotate A at all, exactly, and the change to the effective matrix after one SGD step is 

ΔW₁ = −η(α/r)²·G·AᵀA

which the page checks to the rounding floor. AᵀA is a random positive semi-definite matrix of rank r, drawn before any data was seen, so the first step is the full-matrix gradient passed through a random filter. The card measures how much of the gradient's energy survives that filter, and it tracks r/d loosely and noisily rather than exactly, because A's rows are random and neither orthogonal nor unit-length.

One thing that follows from those two lines is worth separating from the reason usually given for them. B = 0 makes the adapter a no-op you can ship beside a frozen model and merge back exactly, and that is a real property. It is not, on this task, a training requirement: drawing B from a small Gaussian instead changes the final excess by less than the seed spread, and it takes an initial change several times the size of ΔW* itself before the number clearly suffers. The card measures both, so the exact identity and the absent training benefit are visible in the same table. What does cost is freezing A — several times the excess at the same parameter count — which says the win is in A moving, not in where it started.

The rank is not the capacity you think you are buying

The advice that follows from “rank r can express any rank-r change” is to pick r at least as large as the intrinsic rank of the change you need. This page is built to test that, because it makes the intrinsic rank a knob: the downstream task is the base model with a change ΔW* constructed as a product of a d×ρ and a ρ×d matrix, so its rank is exactly ρ and the question “can rank r express it?” has a yes-or-no answer with no experiment in it.

At r ≥ ρ the answer is yes, and the page does not merely assert that — it constructs the adapter. Take the SVD of ΔW*, put its top r right singular vectors into A and its scaled left singular vectors into B, and the adapted model reproduces the teacher to the SVD's own rounding: a weight gap around 1e-14 and an excess around 1e-17, since KL is quadratic in the logit error. So a zero-excess solution exists inside the rank-r family, is writable in closed form, and is where training does not go: at r = ρ the trained adapter lands two orders of magnitude away from it.

And the ordering is not the one the advice predicts. Sweep ρ and the best rank is small and roughly constant — 1 to 3 — and it does not follow ρ. A rank that provably cannot express ΔW* beats a rank that provably can in about four paired runs out of five. What r is doing on this task is not buying expressiveness that gets used; it is limiting how much of the sampled-label noise the model can absorb. The data control is what turns that from a story into a claim: raise the training set from 250 to 16,000 examples and the penalty for the largest rank collapses by most of an order of magnitude — which is what a capacity-versus-noise effect has to do, and what an expressiveness limit could not do.

The honest edge of the claim is in the same card. More data does move the best rank up. It moves it from 1 to 2, and at the largest training set the second-largest rank finishes within a thousandth of the best. The claim is therefore not “small r always wins” but “ρ does not predict the winner, and the gap it is supposed to explain closes with data rather than with rank”.

Why the nearest matrix is not the best model

There is an obvious way to reason about what a rank ceiling costs: take the change you want, find its closest rank-r approximation, and read off the error. Eckart and Young make the first step easy — truncating the SVD gives the closest rank-r matrix in the Frobenius norm, and the page tests that against thousands of random r-dimensional subspaces rather than citing it. The trouble is the norm. Frobenius distance treats every input direction as equally likely, and the layer does not see every input direction equally: it sees whatever the frozen trunk in front of it produces.

That distribution is a fixed matrix here, Σ = E[hhᵀ], because the trunk is frozen and never moves during training. The mean squared change in the layer's own output is then E‖Δh‖² = tr(ΔΣΔᵀ) = ‖ΔL‖² for Σ = LLᵀ, so the best rank-r change for the layer's output is the plain truncation taken in whitened coordinates and mapped back. Two references, each provably optimal in its own norm, each therefore provably worse in the other's. The page asserts both directions of that, and asserts that they become bit-identical when Σ is the identity, which is the control that says the difference is the data and not the code.

Neither of them is a floor on what LoRA achieves, and the first card shows it: at r = 1 the trained adapter's excess sits below the excess of the closest rank-1 approximation of ΔW*. There is no contradiction. The truncation is the closest matrix; the trained adapter is closer to a good function. Nor is the data-weighted reference reliably the better model — aggregated over every intrinsic rank it wins most of the time, and at the page's default ρ the plain one wins two of three, which the card names rather than hides. A surrogate is a surrogate, and the mean squared change in a layer's output is not the loss.

What the trained change looks like

The last piece of the picture is where training actually goes. Measured in the Σ inner product, the change LoRA finds is not a low-rank approximation of ΔW* at all: the cosine between them sits well below 1 at every rank, and the norm of the trained change grows with r until at r = d it is several times the size of the change that defines the task. The adapter is not recovering ΔW*; it is finding some other change that fits the sampled labels, and the excess says the two are comparable functions on the data even though the matrices are far apart.

A's row space does move — the drift column is well below 1 at small r — and it matters: freezing A at its random draw and training only B costs several times the excess at the same parameter count. So the subspace is not simply handed to the model by the random number generator, even though the first step cannot rotate it. That is one place where a plausible story about LoRA does not survive contact with the measurement.

The parameter arithmetic, which is exact

One thing about r is not a measurement at all. An adapter on an m×n matrix trains r(m+n) numbers, the matrix has mn, and the two are equal at r* = mn/(m+n). Above that, LoRA has more trainable parameters than the matrix it is adapting and is strictly less expressive, since a rank-r change is a subset of all changes. On the 12×12 matrix on this page r* is 6, so half the rank axis is in that regime — which is worth knowing when reading the sweep. On real shapes it is nowhere near: 2048 for a 4096×4096 projection, about 2985 for a 4096×11008 feed-forward matrix. That gap is the whole practical case for adapters, and it is arithmetic, not a result.

What a comparison on this page is allowed to claim

The seed moves the initial A and the minibatch order, and it moves the final excess by more than most of the differences an r sweep is about. The floor card measures that directly, and it is why the headline table is paired — same seed both sides, counted — rather than a table of means with a winner circled. Differences smaller than the seed spread are not results, and the unpaired tables on this page are illustrations of one run, labelled as such.

The setup, so the numbers can be checked

The base model is u → tanh(Winu + bin) → tanh(W·h + b) → Wouth' + bout over 6 Gaussian inputs and 4 classes, and the adapted matrix is the middle one. The downstream teacher is that model with W replaced by W + ΔW*, so p*(y|x) is a forward pass. Training uses labels sampled from p*; evaluation uses p* itself, so a model's held-out score is ∑yp*(y|x)·(−log q(y|x)) averaged over held-out x — an exact expectation over labels — and subtracting the conditional entropy turns it into a mean KL that is exactly zero for the teacher and non-negative for everything else. Forward and backward are both written by hand; the gradients are checked against forward-mode automatic differentiation over dual numbers, which is exact, and also against central differences taken on a second forward pass written independently of the first.

🔨 How it is built

1. A task whose answer is a matrix

Every claim here is “rank r is enough” or “this model is better than that one”, so both have to have answers. The downstream teacher is the base model with a change of exact rank ρ applied to one matrix, built as a product of a d×ρ and a ρ×d Gaussian factor. That gives two things at once: p*(y|x) in closed form, so the held-out score is an exact expectation over labels rather than an average of sampled ones; and an algebraic answer to whether a rank-r adapter can express the change, with no experiment involved.

2. The adapter, and the two lines that are its backward pass

forward :  pre = W·h + b + s·B(A·h)          s = α/r
backward:  G = mean over the batch of δ·hᵀ
           dA = s·BᵀG          dB = s·G·Aᵀ

The reverse pass computes the gradient with respect to the effective matrix and the adapter's own gradients are that matrix contracted with the factors, which is not a shortcut — it is what the chain rule gives, and it is why the same code path serves the full fine-tune control by simply using G directly.

3. Two forward passes, one merged and one not

The first forward keeps the branch separate, which is how an adapter really runs. The second merges the adapter into the matrix first, walks its sums in reverse index order, and computes tanh out of exp instead of calling Math.tanh. It shares no line with the first. The two agreeing to about 1e-14 is the page's check that merging an adapter into its base — the thing that makes LoRA free at inference — really is the same model, and that the agreement is a rounding bound rather than an identity is worth saying out loud: the two orders of summation are not the same arithmetic.

4. Gradients against an exact method, not only a finite difference

A central difference has a step size, and a step size means a bias term and a cancellation term, so its best achievable agreement is around 1e-8. The page instead carries a dual number — a value paired with a derivative — down the same path the forward pass takes, which gives the derivative of the loss with respect to one chosen parameter with no step size at all. Forward mode is also the opposite traversal to the backward pass it is checking, so a shared misconception about the graph would have to appear twice, in two directions, to survive. Agreement is around 1e-14, which is the rounding floor rather than a tolerance, and the finite differences are run as well because the claim would be weaker without them.

5. Hand-written linear algebra: Jacobi, Cholesky, and an honest resolution limit

The SVD is built from a cyclic Jacobi eigendecomposition of MᵀM: rotations only, so the singular vectors come out orthogonal to the rounding floor rather than by construction. The data-weighted truncation needs a Cholesky factor of Σ and its triangular inverse, both written out. Going through the Gram matrix squares the singular values, so a mathematically zero singular value resolves at roughly the square root of machine epsilon — about 1e-8, not 1e-16 — and the page prints that ratio in its own column and uses 1e-6 as the rank threshold instead of pretending a rank test is exact.

6. Eckart-Young, tested rather than cited

The claim that truncating the SVD gives the closest rank-r matrix is load-bearing for two of the cards, so it is checked: draw a random r-dimensional row space, project M onto it exactly — which gives the best matrix with that row space, no optimisation needed — and keep the best of a few thousand draws. It never beats the truncation, and the margin widens with r, because a random subspace in twelve dimensions is a bad guess and gets worse.

7. The controls, and why they are the point

Five parameterisations of the same change share one training loop: the adapter, the adapter with A frozen, a random subset of W's entries with exactly the adapter's parameter count, W's diagonal, and the whole matrix. The parameter-matched random subset is the control that decides whether the structure earns anything or whether the count was doing the work. Only the middle matrix is ever trainable in any of them, so nothing else can explain a difference.

8. Paired seeds, and a noise floor measured first

The rank sweep is repeated across seeds and compared seed by seed, and the seed spread is measured separately so the reader can see which differences are allowed to mean anything. The held-out sample spread is measured too, and it is much smaller, because removing label sampling from the evaluation removes the dominant term.

9. Everything runs in the page

One file, no build step, no dependency, no network. The engine lives between two markers so it can be lifted out and run under Node against independently written oracles: a different generator, a different Gaussian transform, a forward pass built the other way round, an explicit Jacobian, forward-mode duals against the page's reverse mode, a one-sided Jacobi SVD against the page's Gram-matrix one, and a linear twin of the model with the nonlinearity removed, where the gradient is a closed form and no backward pass is involved at all. The assertion block at the bottom of the MEASURE tab is the same suite the page ships with, run in the browser on the numbers actually on screen.
