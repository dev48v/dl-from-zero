DEEPLEARNINGFROMZERO · DAY 65

⚖️ AdamW — weight decay and L2 regularisation are the same thing under SGD, and not the same thing under Adam. That one sentence is the whole paper (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) and it is why torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) does not do what its name suggests. Add an L2 term to the loss and the penalty goes through Adam's per-parameter normaliser — so it is divided by √v̂, which means the parameters with the largest gradients get the least decay, exactly backwards from what regularisation is for. Decouple it, apply the decay straight to the weight, and the effective decay is uniform again. This page implements both optimisers by hand, trains a real model in your browser, and measures the thing rather than describing it: the effective decay per parameter under each, the resulting weight-norm trajectories, and the coupling that makes L2-under-Adam's regularisation strength change when you change the learning rate. Gradients are checked against finite differences, and the SGD identity — where the two really are equivalent — is asserted rather than assumed. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, offline.

The spread is what makes the difference visible: real networks have parameters whose gradients differ by orders of magnitude, and that is precisely the axis along which L2-under-Adam and true weight decay diverge.

THE COUPLING: λ AND THE LEARNING RATE

Under AdamW the shrink per step is exactly lr·λ, so halving the learning rate halves the regularisation — which is why AdamW's λ needs retuning with the schedule. Under Adam+L2 the relationship is not even monotone in a useful way, because the penalty is normalised by a running gradient statistic that also moves.

UNDER SGD THEY ARE THE SAME THING

The identity the whole story rests on. With plain SGD, adding λw to the gradient and multiplying the weight by (1 − lr·λ) produce bit-identical updates. Measured over 400 steps: 


          

            That equivalence is exactly what breaks when the update is rescaled per parameter, and
            it is why the confusion existed for years without anyone noticing.
          

        

      


    

  






  

    


      
Two things everyone treats as synonyms


      
L2 regularisation adds a penalty to the loss:

      
L'(w) = L(w) + (λ/2)‖w‖²     ⟹     ∇L'(w) = ∇L(w) + λw


      
Weight decay does not touch the loss. It shrinks the weight directly, as
      part of the update:

      
w ← (1 − lr·λ) · w  −  lr · ∇L(w)


      
Under plain SGD, expand both and they are the same update. Identical. Which is why the two
      terms became synonyms, why frameworks named the L2 knob weight_decay, and why
      nobody looked again for twenty years.


      
Adam breaks the equivalence


      
Adam does not apply the gradient. It applies the gradient divided by a running estimate
      of its own magnitude:


      
w ← w − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)


      
Put the L2 term in the gradient and it goes through that division too:


      
effective decay ≈ lr · λ·w / (√v̂ + ε)


      

        
The decay is now divided by the gradient magnitude. A
        parameter with large, consistent gradients has a large √v̂, so it gets
        less regularisation. A parameter that barely moves gets more. That is exactly
        backwards from what regularisation is supposed to do, and the ratio between the two is
        measurable — this page prints it.

      


      
AdamW's fix is a one-line move: take the decay out of the gradient and apply it to the
      weight, after the adaptive step.


      
w ← w − lr · m̂/(√v̂ + ε)  −  lr · λ · w
//                            ^ decoupled: never sees the normaliser


      
Now every parameter shrinks by the same proportion per step, whatever its gradient looks
      like. That is the whole paper.


      
Why it took until 2017


      
Adam's adaptivity is usually described as its selling point: parameters get their own
      effective learning rate. That is true and useful for the gradient. Nobody noticed it
      was also silently applied to the penalty, because L2 and weight decay had been
      interchangeable for so long that the distinction had stopped being a distinction.


      
Loshchilov and Hutter's contribution was mostly noticing. The fix is four characters of
      code moved to a different line.


      
What changes in practice


      
    
        
  • λ needs retuning. AdamW's λ is not comparable to Adam's
        weight_decay. Typical AdamW values are much larger — 0.01 to 0.1 where Adam+L2
        used 1e-4 — because the decay is no longer divided by anything.
    • 
        
  • λ couples to the learning rate. AdamW shrinks by exactly
        lr·λ per step, so a decaying schedule decays your regularisation too. Some
        implementations normalise λ by the schedule for this reason.
    • 
        
  • Do not decay everything. Biases, LayerNorm and BatchNorm scales are
        conventionally excluded — shrinking a normalisation scale toward zero fights the layer's
        purpose, and there are few enough of them that they cannot overfit anyway.
    • 
        
  • Check which one your framework runs. In PyTorch,
        Adam(weight_decay=λ) is the coupled L2 version; AdamW is the
        decoupled one. Same argument name, different algorithm.
    • 
      


      
And the honest part


      
Decoupling is not free improvement in every setting. It is a genuinely different
      regulariser, so a λ tuned for one is wrong for the other, and a comparison that reuses the
      same λ is measuring the retuning rather than the method. The tables on this page hold λ fixed
      deliberately — to show the mechanism, not to declare a winner on a benchmark that
      would need a sweep to be fair.


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. Both optimisers, differing in one line

      
function step(p, g, s, cfg){
  if (cfg.decoupled === false) g = g + cfg.lambda * p;      // L2: into the GRADIENT

  s.m = cfg.b1 * s.m + (1 - cfg.b1) * g;
  s.v = cfg.b2 * s.v + (1 - cfg.b2) * g * g;
  const mHat = s.m / (1 - Math.pow(cfg.b1, s.t));
  const vHat = s.v / (1 - Math.pow(cfg.b2, s.t));

  let next = p - cfg.lr * mHat / (Math.sqrt(vHat) + cfg.eps);

  if (cfg.decoupled === true) next -= cfg.lr * cfg.lambda * p;   // decay: onto the WEIGHT
  return next;
}

      
Two ifs around identical arithmetic. That is genuinely the entire difference
      between Adam(weight_decay=) and AdamW, and writing them as one
      function is the clearest way to see it — there is nowhere else for a difference to hide.


      
2. Bias correction, which is not optional here

      
const mHat = s.m / (1 - Math.pow(cfg.b1, s.t));
const vHat = s.v / (1 - Math.pow(cfg.b2, s.t));

      
m and v start at zero, so early steps are biased toward zero and
      √v̂ is far too small. Skip the correction and the first few dozen steps take
      enormous strides — which would dominate exactly the comparison this page is making. The tests
      assert that vHat at t=1 equals g² exactly, which is the cleanest way
      to catch an off-by-one in the exponent.


      
3. The effective decay, measured not derived

      
// run one step with lambda, one without, and take the difference. Whatever the
// optimiser did to the penalty is in that gap - no algebra required.
const withDecay    = step(p, g, clone(state), { ...cfg, lambda });
const withoutDecay = step(p, g, clone(state), { ...cfg, lambda: 0 });
const effective    = withoutDecay - withDecay;

      
An ablation rather than a formula. If the closed form and the measurement disagree, the
      measurement is what the optimiser is actually doing — and the test checks them against each
      other, so a divergence surfaces instead of being argued about.


      
4. The SGD identity, asserted to the bit

      
// L2 into the gradient
a = a - lr * (grad(a) + lambda * a);
// decay onto the weight
b = b * (1 - lr * lambda) - lr * grad(b);

assert(Math.abs(a - b) < 1e-12);     // over 400 steps, every step

      
This is the load-bearing test of the page. If that identity does not hold, the claim
      "they are the same under SGD and different under Adam" is half wrong and the whole article
      collapses. Asserting the part you expect to be true is as important as asserting the
      part you expect to break.


      
5. A model with a deliberate gradient spread

      
// features scaled across orders of magnitude, so parameters get very different |g|
for (let j = 0; j < d; j++) scale[j] = Math.pow(spread, j / (d - 1));

      
On a well-conditioned problem the two optimisers look nearly identical, which is why the
      difference went unnoticed for so long. The spread is the thing that makes the mechanism
      visible, and it is a slider so you can watch the gap close as you turn it down to 1×.


      
6. Gradients checked against finite differences

      
const numeric = (loss(w + h·e_i) - loss(w - h·e_i)) / (2h);
assert(relativeError(analytic[i], numeric) < 1e-6);

      
Central differences, not forward — the forward version's error is O(h) and would need a
      tolerance loose enough to hide a real bug. Every claim on this page rests on the gradients
      being right, so they are checked first and separately.
